Cleaning Must Haves

The cleaning supplies and tools we actually reach for — pulled from our own detailing routine and every DIY guide on the site, gathered in one place.

Vegan Leather Cleaner

This is formulated specifically for Tesla's vegan leather, not a generic all-purpose cleaner, and it shows in how gentle it is on the material. A little goes a long way — you really only need a couple sprays per section. Spray it onto a microfiber cloth first rather than directly on the seat, then wipe clean; that's the method that's worked best for me. Keeps the seats looking new without any of the dryness or fading some cleaners cause over time.

Leather Shield (Ceramic)

This applies easily and dries fast, which matters a lot with a white vegan leather interior — one swipe and it's not sitting there waiting to attract dust like some sealants do. It's become the natural pair to the Vegan Leather Cleaner: clean the seats, then seal them, and stains from juice boxes and snack messes wipe up instead of soaking in. A little goes a long way with both products, so a small bottle lasts way longer than you'd expect. With two kids in the back seat, this combo has saved the interior more times than I can count.

View on Amazon

Microfiber Drying Towel

This is a straightforward drying towel, and that's really all it needs to be — it pulls most of the water off the exterior fast enough to actually beat water spots forming. The method is dead simple: get it wet, wring it out, glide it across the body, wring it out again, repeat. No special technique required, just don't skip the wringing or you're just pushing water around instead of lifting it.

Chenille Microfiber Wash Mitt

I'm a big fan of Chemical Guys products in general — they're reasonably priced for the quality you get, and I'd rather buy once and cry once than keep replacing cheaper stuff every few months. What stood out with this mitt specifically is that even on the first use, it didn't leave any of those little fuzzies on the paint that some of the cheaper mitts I've used before always seemed to shed. Small detail, but it's the kind of thing that tells you the quality is actually there.

Car Wash Soap

Another solid one from Chemical Guys. I like that it works both ways — through a foam cannon or just dumped straight into a wash bucket — so there's no need to keep two different soaps around depending on how you're washing that day. It lathers well, smells good, and leaves a noticeably nice shine once it's rinsed off.

Pack of Microfiber Towels

You can find these just about anywhere, but I've noticed online shops tend to carry bigger packs than what you'll see in a store, which is worth it since these get used on literally everything. I keep a handful in the car — one in each door compartment, a few in the trunk — plus more out in the garage. You genuinely can't have too many of these lying around.

Glass Cleaner Spray

I've tried a handful of glass cleaners over the years, and for whatever reason this one consistently leaves fewer streaks than the others I've used. It does have a bit of a smell to it when you first spray it, but it fades quickly and isn't something that lingers. I'll take this over Windex every time at this point.

Portable Duster/Vacuum

This thing is small but the suction is way stronger than I expected for something this size, and the blower mode is just as handy. It's become my go-to for cleaning up whatever mess the kids leave behind — crumbs, cracker dust, the usual chaos. I've also used the blower side to clear debris out of the frunk and trunk compartments. Compact, actually useful, and it's earned a permanent spot in the car.

CAR GUYS Plastic Restorer

The black trim on this car fades embarrassingly fast if you don't stay on top of it, and this is the only restorer I've used that doesn't just mask the problem for a week. It goes on easily without leaving a greasy residue, and the finish actually holds up through rain instead of streaking off after the first wash. A little goes a long way, so the bottle's lasted way longer than I expected for the price. It brought back trim I genuinely thought was too far gone to save. I keep a bottle in the garage now and just touch it up every month or so.

Car Jack

This is the jack I actually use for tire rotations in the garage — no need to book a service appointment just to swap corners. It's rated well above what the Model Y needs, so there's margin for error, and it lifts smoothly without the jerky pumping some cheaper jacks have. Pair it with the correct jack pads and you've got everything needed for a full DIY tire rotation.

Tesla Jack Pads / Lift Pucks

Tesla's battery pack runs along the underside of the car, so a standard jack puck in the wrong spot risks real damage. These are shaped to sit exactly on the Model Y's factory lift points, so you're not guessing where it's safe to jack. I keep a set in the frunk permanently now — cheap insurance for something you really don't want to get wrong.

Torque Wrench

Wheel bolts have a real torque spec, and eyeballing “tight enough” with a regular ratchet is how you end up either loosening over time or stripping the threads. This one's easy to set and clicks right at the correct torque, no guessing involved. If you're doing your own tire rotations, this is the one tool I wouldn't skip.

21mm Socket

The Model Y's factory wheel bolts take a 21mm socket, which isn't always the size that comes in a generic set. This one's a straightforward, no-frills fit that pairs directly with the torque wrench above — no adapters, no rounding off the bolt heads.

Plastic Trim Tool

Half the interior and frunk panels on this car are held on with plastic clips, and a screwdriver will crack them if you're not careful. This tool is shaped to get under trim without marking or snapping anything — I've used it for the cabin filter swap, the frunk filter swap, and the radiator cleaning job without a single broken clip.
