The displayed range is an estimate based on EPA-rated energy consumption, not a prediction of how far you'll actually drive. Weather, speed, terrain, HVAC use, tire pressure, cargo weight, and driving style all affect real-world range. It's also normal to see a small amount of battery capacity loss during the first several months of ownership, followed by much slower long-term degradation. For a more accurate prediction for your current trip, use the Energy app in the vehicle, which calculates range based on your recent driving conditions instead of EPA estimates.

Regenerative Braking is the process of using the electric motors to slow the vehicle while recovering energy and sending it back into the battery. One-Pedal Driving is the driving experience created by strong regenerative braking — when you lift your foot off the accelerator, the vehicle slows significantly, often letting you drive without using the brake pedal for most normal stops. Regenerative braking increases driving range, reduces brake wear, and automatically blends in the conventional brakes if regenerative braking is limited (such as when the battery is cold or fully charged).

Regenerative braking is a system that slows the car down and recharges the battery by turning the electric motor into a generator when you lift your foot off the accelerator. Per Tesla, whenever the car is moving and your foot is off the accelerator, regenerative braking slows the vehicle and feeds surplus power back to the battery, and anticipating your stops this way can increase your driving range.

A used Tesla is different from a used gas car — there's no engine to listen to, but there's a lot more hiding in software logs and hardware specs than most buyers think to check. Here's what actually matters.

Paperwork & History

Confirm the VIN matches the dash, door jamb sticker, title, and registration — any mismatch is a walk-away. Run the VIN through NHTSA's NMVTIS database (vehiclehistory.gov) plus a Carfax or AutoCheck report for accident history, a clean title, and mileage discrepancies. Check for open recalls through both NHTSA's recall database and Tesla's own recall lookup using the VIN, since they don't always show identical results. If possible, ask the seller for maintenance and service history — this isn't visible anywhere inside the car itself, so it really comes down to what the seller is willing to share and confirm.

Battery Health

There's a faster way to spot-check this than charging all the way to 100%, which usually isn't practical when you're standing in front of a car you're considering. Look at the current state of charge on the screen, tap the percentage to switch it to miles, then divide the miles shown by that percentage (as a decimal) to estimate the rated range at a full charge — compare that against the car's original EPA-rated range when new. A drop of around 10% or less after several years and tens of thousands of miles is fairly normal. Tesla's own stated threshold for a warranty battery replacement is 30% degradation, which is a pretty generous bar — worth knowing, but don't treat "under 30%" as automatically fine on its own; weigh it against the car's actual age and mileage. Beyond the quick check, a third-party app like Tessie or TeslaFi can estimate battery health from charging history, and the most thorough option is a full battery test run from Service Mode on the touchscreen (more on that below), which drains and tests the pack directly. Degradation is normal on every Tesla and happens fastest early on — often most of it in the first 20,000-30,000 miles before flattening out significantly, so a heavily-degraded-looking low-mileage car isn't necessarily a bad sign. It's worth asking how the car was charged, too: cars charged mostly at Superchargers can degrade slightly faster than ones charged mostly at home, though this really just comes down to trusting the seller's word, since it's not something you can verify yourself.

Accident History & Battery Replacement

Beyond a standard vehicle history report, ask specifically whether the battery pack itself (not the small 12V battery up front) has ever been replaced — cars with one replacement seem more likely to need another down the line. One thing that catches people off guard: a car with a rebuilt or salvage title can lose access to the Supercharger network. This doesn't always come back — some salvage cars can be reinstated after Tesla inspects and clears the car as safe to fast-charge, but for others, Supercharging access is gone permanently with no path to restore it. Either way, confirm directly whether a car with any accident history can still Supercharge before you buy — this alone can significantly affect what the car is worth to you, especially if you road-trip.

Autopilot / FSD Hardware & Software

Hardware and software are two different things. Software (an FSD purchase) is tied to the account or VIN; hardware (the cameras and computer) is physical and generally can't be upgraded. Check the hardware version on the touchscreen under Controls → Software → Additional Vehicle Information. Rough cutoffs: Model S and X switched from HW3 to HW4 after March 2023, Model Y after May 2023, and all 2024-and-later Model 3 (the refreshed "Highland") come with HW4 — anything older is likely HW3, and an older S or X could even be HW2.5. Within HW3 specifically, there are two different processor variants — an older Intel-based one and a newer AMD Ryzen-based one — and the newer Ryzen cars have generally kept getting more software features than the Intel ones, even though both are technically "HW3." That same Vehicle Information screen tells you which one a car has. Tesla has said, informally and not in writing, that HW3 cars would get a free upgrade to HW4 if full autonomy ends up requiring it, but that's not a guarantee to rely on. For FSD: if it was purchased outright by a previous owner, it stays with the car and transfers to you; if it was a subscription, it doesn't carry over. One more thing worth confirming directly with the seller: Tesla has occasionally run promotions letting owners transfer FSD to a different car before selling, so if a listing mentions FSD, confirm the seller isn't planning to move it off before the sale finalizes.

Feature Verification

A couple of trim-specific features are easy to miss and worth checking in that same Software → Vehicle Information screen: whether the car has Premium Audio (varies by year and trim, and affects which speakers are active), and whether a Tow Package is actually enabled in software — even if you can physically see a tow hook, an aftermarket hitch may not have the towing software turned on, which matters if towing is part of why you're buying the car.

Exterior & Body

Check panel gaps and alignment for anything inconsistent, which can point to a factory defect or prior collision repair. Look over the glass roof for "starburst" cracks from rock chips, edge cracks, or hazy spots — replacement runs $1,500 to $3,000. Give the front bumper and hood a close look too — Tesla's paint has a reputation for being on the softer side, especially on older cars, so chips tend to show up there first. Watch for mismatched paint texture or overspray, and check the lower rear door area closely, especially on a Model Y — it takes more road debris than you'd expect. If any part of the car has clear paint protection film, especially on those lower rear doors, that's generally a good sign the previous owner took care of it. Windshields seem a little more prone to rock chips too. If the car has factory wheel covers, know that they sit slightly higher than aftermarket ones, which can hide curb rash underneath — common on former rental-fleet cars. While you're down there, check underneath the car — it should be a mostly flat surface, and any large gashes or dents near the battery area are worth asking about, since that's a sign of possible impact damage to the pack itself. Do a penny test on the tires, and don't rely on mileage alone to judge tire life — real-world wear on Teslas varies a lot, so check the actual tread yourself. Check the brake rotors for pitting too — Tesla brakes barely get used thanks to regenerative braking, so rotors can actually rust from disuse rather than wear out.

Suspension & Drivetrain (Test Drive)

Drive over speed bumps and listen for clunking, which usually means worn control arm bushings or sway bar links, common past 60,000 miles. A crunching or creaking noise during tight, low-speed turns can point to a failing front compression rod bushing. Accelerate from 0-30 mph and listen for whining or grinding — a whine that scales with speed rather than throttle input suggests drive unit bearing wear, most common between 30,000 and 80,000 miles. Lift off the accelerator at speed and confirm regenerative braking feels smooth, not pulsing or noisy.

Service Mode: The Hidden Diagnostic Tool

Every Tesla has a built-in diagnostic mode most buyers never think to check — and sellers may not even know something's flagged. To get in: go to the car menu → Software, then hold your finger on the car's logo for a few seconds — you'll be prompted for a password, which is simply "service." A few things worth checking once you're in: look at the active alerts list — most items tend to be minor, but pay attention to anything related to the drivetrain, battery, or HVAC system, and photograph anything unclear so you can look it up later. Check the main diagnostic screens for HVAC, high/low voltage, and sensor/camera status — you don't need to understand everything, but a mostly-green screen is a good sign. Use Service → Camera Preview to confirm every camera is actually working. If you can, test-drive to a Supercharger and confirm the car accepts a charge without issue. Test the car's Wi-Fi by tethering it to your phone and confirming you can reach the internet, since Teslas need working Wi-Fi to receive updates. Turn on the HVAC system and test both heat and cold, the seat heaters, and the steering wheel heater, and listen for any rattles. One thing Service Mode won't show you: maintenance history isn't visible anywhere in the car, so that part you'll just have to take the seller's word on.

Interior & Electronics

Open and close every door, the frunk, and the trunk both manually and via the touchscreen — all of it runs through electronic systems, so a fault here can be a costly repair. Test all windows, mirrors, the center console, and glovebox. Confirm you're getting both physical key cards a new Tesla ships with, not just a phone key. Also make sure the previous owner actually removes the car from their Tesla account and transfers it to yours, or your phone won't work as a key even if everything else checks out.

Charging

Test an actual charge if you can, ideally on both a home-style connector and a Supercharger. Teslas produced or purchased after April 17, 2022 don't include a charging cable, so don't assume one's included. If the car is older, it's also worth checking CCS compatibility — the charging standard used by most non-Tesla fast-charging networks like Electrify America. Most newer Teslas support it out of the box; if an older one doesn't, it's not a dealbreaker, since Tesla can retrofit it for a few hundred dollars. On the home-charging side, you likely don't need an electrical upgrade at all — a regular 120V outlet with a Mobile Connector is often enough for overnight charging. If you do want a faster home setup with a proper Wall Connector, check what your garage's panel can actually support first, since upgrading a circuit for it can run into the thousands of dollars depending on your home.

Warranty & Ownership

You can check total mileage on the touchscreen under Trips, in Settings — worth doing directly rather than trusting a listing. The Basic Vehicle Limited Warranty runs 4 years or 50,000 miles from the original in-service date. The Battery and Drive Unit Warranty runs 8 years, with mileage varying by trim: 100,000 miles for Model 3/Y RWD, 120,000 for Performance and Premium AWD trims, 150,000 for Model S/X. Buying directly from Tesla also comes with a Used Vehicle Limited Warranty covering the remainder of the basic warranty, plus a bonus year or 10,000 miles after that expires. Private-party and third-party dealer sales don't come with any additional Tesla warranty beyond what's already left on the original terms.
