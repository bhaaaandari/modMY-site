No. Tesla vehicles are designed to use both AC charging and DC Supercharging safely. The Battery Management System automatically monitors battery temperature, charging rate, and state of charge to protect the battery during fast charging. Frequent DC fast charging may result in slightly more battery wear over many years compared to primarily using Level 2 charging, but Tesla considers Supercharging a normal part of vehicle operation. For the fastest charging experience, navigate to the Supercharger so the battery can automatically precondition before you arrive. Some gradual battery degradation over time is normal regardless of charging method.

The displayed range is an estimate based on EPA-rated energy consumption, not a prediction of how far you'll actually drive. Weather, speed, terrain, HVAC use, tire pressure, cargo weight, and driving style all affect real-world range. It's also normal to see a small amount of battery capacity loss during the first several months of ownership, followed by much slower long-term degradation. For a more accurate prediction for your current trip, use the Energy app in the vehicle, which calculates range based on your recent driving conditions instead of EPA estimates.

Regenerative Braking is the process of using the electric motors to slow the vehicle while recovering energy and sending it back into the battery. One-Pedal Driving is the driving experience created by strong regenerative braking — when you lift your foot off the accelerator, the vehicle slows significantly, often letting you drive without using the brake pedal for most normal stops. Regenerative braking increases driving range, reduces brake wear, and automatically blends in the conventional brakes if regenerative braking is limited (such as when the battery is cold or fully charged).

A used Tesla is different from a used gas car — there's no engine to listen to, but there's a lot more hiding in software logs and hardware specs than most buyers think to check. Here's what actually matters.

Paperwork & History

Confirm the VIN matches the dash, door jamb sticker, title, and registration — any mismatch is a walk-away. Run the VIN through NHTSA's NMVTIS database (vehiclehistory.gov) plus a Carfax or AutoCheck report for accident history and mileage discrepancies. Check for open recalls through both NHTSA's recall database and Tesla's own recall lookup using the VIN, since they don't always show identical results. If possible, ask the seller for service history through the Tesla app — repeated visits for the same issue is a bigger red flag than several different minor ones.

Battery Health

The simplest free check: charge to 100%, switch the display to miles, and compare it against the EPA-rated range for that model, year, and trim. More than roughly 10-15% below the rated number is worth questioning. In the Tesla app, Request Service → Battery & Charging → Range will compare the car against expected degradation curves. Most Teslas lose only 3-5% capacity in the first 10,000 miles, then degradation slows down significantly — many cars still show 88-92% capacity around 100,000 miles. Anything far outside that curve is worth asking about.

Autopilot / FSD Hardware & Software

Hardware and software are two different things. Software (an FSD purchase) is tied to the account or VIN; hardware (the cameras and computer) is physical and generally can't be upgraded. Check the hardware version on the touchscreen under Controls → Software → Additional Vehicle Information. Roughly speaking, HW3 covers 2019 and later, HW4 covers most cars built after January 2023, and Tesla has said there's no HW3-to-HW4 upgrade path — this affects both resale value and future FSD eligibility. Ask directly whether FSD or Enhanced Autopilot is tied to the VIN (it transfers with the car) or the seller's account (it doesn't).

Exterior & Body

Check panel gaps and alignment for anything inconsistent, which can point to a factory defect or prior collision repair. Look over the glass roof for "starburst" cracks from rock chips, edge cracks, or hazy spots — replacement runs $1,500 to $3,000. Watch for mismatched paint texture or overspray. Do a penny test on the tires for tread depth, and look for uneven wear, which can point to alignment or suspension issues. Check the brake rotors for pitting too — Tesla brakes barely get used thanks to regenerative braking, so rotors can actually rust from disuse rather than wear out.

Suspension & Drivetrain (Test Drive)

Drive over speed bumps and listen for clunking, which usually means worn control arm bushings or sway bar links, common past 60,000 miles. A crunching or creaking noise during tight, low-speed turns can point to a failing front compression rod bushing. Accelerate from 0-30 mph and listen for whining or grinding — a whine that scales with speed rather than throttle input suggests drive unit bearing wear, most common between 30,000 and 80,000 miles. Lift off the accelerator at speed and confirm regenerative braking feels smooth, not pulsing or noisy.

Interior & Electronics

Open and close every door, the frunk, and the trunk both manually and via the touchscreen. Test all windows, mirrors, the center console, and glovebox. Check every light works, including looking for moisture inside the housings. Confirm all keys or key cards actually work.

Charging

Test an actual charge if you can, ideally on both a home-style connector and a Supercharger. One thing that catches people off guard: Teslas produced or purchased after April 17, 2022 don't include a charging cable, so don't assume one's included — confirm it directly.

Warranty & Ownership

The Basic Vehicle Limited Warranty runs 4 years or 50,000 miles from the original in-service date — check how much is left. The Battery and Drive Unit Warranty runs 8 years, though mileage varies: 100,000 miles for Model 3/Y RWD, 120,000 for Performance and Premium AWD trims, 150,000 for Model S/X. This is the warranty that matters most for a used purchase. Buying directly from Tesla also comes with a Used Vehicle Limited Warranty — the remainder of the basic warranty, plus a bonus year or 10,000 miles after that expires. Private-party and third-party dealer sales don't come with any additional Tesla warranty beyond what's already left on the original terms, which is worth factoring into your offer.
